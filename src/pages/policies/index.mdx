Most of these are the ordinary rules of any course. Two of them are peculiar to
this one, and they come first.

## Do not play the specimen before week ten

You get the rules of Close Quarters in week one. The game itself is linked from
the home page from the first day, on purpose, and you are asked not to open it
before the [week ten](/lectures/week-10/) tutorial.

Nobody can enforce this and nobody will try. It is here because the course only
works once: if you play first, you spend nine weeks defending your first
impression, and the week ten tutorial has nothing left to show you. If you have
already played it, say so on your first quiz. That is useful data and
it costs you nothing.

## Every number carries its papers

A number in this course is reported with its unit, the number of runs behind
it, the seeds or the command that reproduces it, and the machine and program it
ran in, because the same seeds can end differently in Node and in a browser, or
on another computer (week 2). The working run count is 51. This applies to
assessed work, to the quizzes, and to what you say out loud in a tutorial.

The course's seeds are 97, 194, 291 and on, each 97 past the last. Tutorials
count them, so "seeds 1 to 51" means 97 to 4947, and week 2's seed 97 is seed 1.
"Ordinary card choices" means the player takes the leftmost of the three cards
it is offered.

Three things are treated as not having been submitted at all:

- a single run quoted as if it were a result
- the best run, or the run you happened to watch
- a number that cannot be reproduced from your fork

This is the only rule in the course that is about integrity and method at the
same time.

## Late work and extensions

Extensions are easy to get and are arranged **before** the deadline, by email
to [Tamsin](/people/tamsin-okoro/). You do not need to explain much. After the
deadline there is nothing to extend.

[Tutorial quizzes](/assessments/tutorial-quizzes/) are sat in the room at the
end of the tutorial they belong to. There is no make-up quiz; the two lowest of
the twelve are dropped instead, so two missed weeks cost nothing.

## Your own work, and help from others

Look at each other's screens. Argue about each other's numbers. That is what
the tutorials are for. Your fork, your quiz answers and your defence are your own.

You may use an AI coding tool to write policy functions and measurement
scripts, the same as any other tool, and you should say that you did. Two
limits: you must be able to explain every line of your simulated player when
asked, and a number is never evidence because a tool said it. It is evidence
because the command that produces it runs on someone else's machine.

Inventing a measurement, or editing one to make a change look better, is
academic misconduct and will be treated as such.

## If something about the course is a barrier

Tell [Tamsin](/people/tamsin-okoro/) early, in whatever detail you are
comfortable with.

Two things are already in place. The final defence is a recording, so it can be
made in as many takes as you need. And the week ten tutorial has an observer's
version: if ten minutes of fast play is not possible or not pleasant for you,
you sit beside someone who is playing and write the list from what you see. It
produces the same kind of findings and is marked the same way.

## Who to ask

The person next to you in the tutorial, then [Wei](/people/wei-lindqvist/), then
[Tamsin](/people/tamsin-okoro/). For anything that should not wait, email
either of them and put the course code in the subject line.
